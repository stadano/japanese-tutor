Karst mountains, terraced valleys, and a lot of walking. This is a guide to the north in the best week of its year — what's out there, how far it is, and which combinations actually work in eight days.

Four ways to do it

Worked routes

Each of these is a block you can drop into the week, with Hanoi as the hinge on either side. Roughly in order: the first is the easiest to pull off, the second the most spectacular, the third the most weather-proof, and the fourth the best-known.

Pù Luông and Mai Châu

4 days · 4h out

The best fit if the priority is walking. October catches the tail of the second rice harvest here, and the trekking is valley-to-valley rather than summit-bagging — you walk through farmland and villages rather than past them.

Day 1

Hanoi to Pù Luông

Leave early; it's about four hours by private car. Arrive for lunch. Ease in with the Bản Đôn valley loop — five or six flat kilometres through the paddies and past the bamboo waterwheels. Good at either end of the day.

Day 2

Kho Mường and the Bat Cave

The main trek: roughly ten kilometres over a ridge and down into a hidden valley, finishing at Hang Dơi. Moderate, and worth a local guide — a lot of the trails aren't on any map.

Day 3

Hiếu village and waterfall

A gentler valley walk to a run of limestone cascades with pools you can swim in. Afternoon free. If it's a Sunday, the Phố Đoàn market is worth building the day around.

Day 4

Back via Mai Châu

Break the return in the Mai Châu valley — flat, ringed by mountains, and ideal for a few hours on a rented bicycle before the last stretch into Hanoi.

Hà Giang or Cao Bằng

4 days · 7h out

The most dramatic landscape in the country, and the biggest commitment. Overnight buses each way are standard. Hà Giang has the famous passes and the crowds that come with them; Cao Bằng has smoother roads, bigger water, and far fewer people.

Night 1

Sleeper bus north

Around seven hours, leaving Hanoi in the evening and arriving before dawn. Book a limousine van rather than a bus berth if you'd like to arrive functional.

Day 1

Into the mountains

Hà Giang: north through Quản Bạ toward Yên Minh or Du Già. Cao Bằng: out to Nguyên Bình and the Phia Oắc highlands. Either way, hire a driver or a local easy-rider — the roads are the attraction and you'll see more from the back.

Day 2

The set piece

Hà Giang: Đồng Văn, Lũng Cú at the northern tip, then the Mã Pí Lèng pass above the Nho Quế gorge. Cao Bằng: Bản Giốc waterfall, at high post-monsoon flow but with the water already cleared to turquoise.

Day 3

Walk, then turn south

There's real hiking here beyond the roads — down into the gorge, or between the villages around Du Già and Khuổi Ky. Evening bus back to Hanoi.

Note

Border permits

Foreign visitors need a Border Area Entry Permit for Hà Giang's northern districts — Quản Bạ, Yên Minh, Đồng Văn and Mèo Vạc. It's about 250,000₫, arranged in Hà Giang City, and now checked at roadblocks. Any decent operator includes it; get that confirmed in writing.

Cát Bà, Lan Hạ and Hải Phòng

3 days · 3h out

Jungle trekking, sea kayaking, and a colonial port city almost nobody stops in. The shortest travel times of the three, which also makes it the fallback if a storm system shows up in the forecast.

Day 1

Hải Phòng

Two hours from Hanoi by road. Wide boulevards, a French opera house, and some of the best regional food in the north — bánh đa cua, bánh mì cay, chả chìa. An evening of walking is the whole point.

Day 2

Cát Bà National Park

Speedboat across in under an hour. Trek up to Ngũ Lâm peak for the view over the archipelago, or take the longer crossing through the park to Ao Ếch, the island's freshwater lake.

Day 3

Lan Hạ Bay

A full day on the water — boat out, then kayaks through the lagoons and sea caves, with lunch at Việt Hải, a village inside the park you can only reach by boat or bike. Same karst as Ha Long, a fraction of the traffic.

Sapa and the Mường Hoa valley

4 days · overnight train

The distance is the objection people raise, and the sleeper train mostly answers it — you travel while asleep instead of losing a day to the road. The other objection, that Sapa is overrun, is fair about the town and wrong about the valley. Stay down among the terraces and it's a different trip.

Night 1

Sleeper train to Lào Cai

Leaves Hanoi late evening, arrives around dawn, roughly eight hours in a four-berth cabin. Book a soft sleeper. From Lào Cai it's about an hour by road up to the valley.

Day 1

Down into the valley

Skip the town and go straight to Tả Van or Lao Chải. Drop bags, then walk the terraces in the afternoon — the paths run village to village along the valley floor and up the sides.

Day 2

The long trek

A full day, 12–15km, through Hmong and Dao villages and across the Mường Hoa terraces. Guides are easy to arrange locally and worth it; a lot of the good routes aren't obvious.

Day 3

Fansipan, or a second valley

Either take the cable car up Indochina's highest mountain — 3,143m, a few minutes each way — or stay low and walk a different valley. Evening train back, arriving Hanoi early the next morning.

Note

Timing

The Sapa harvest peaks mid-September, so early October catches the tail of it. Terraces will be cut in places and gold in others rather than uniformly gold. Pù Luông, with its later second harvest, is the better bet if that's the specific thing you're after.

On two wheels

Cycling

Bikes are cheap and widely rentable, and four areas in the north are genuinely good riding — flat or rolling, lightly trafficked, and scenic the whole way. Skip the national highways entirely; Highway 1A in particular is no place for a bicycle.

Ninh Bình EASY · FLAT

The best of the four. Paved back lanes thread between limestone towers and flooded paddies, linking Tam Cốc, Bích Động and the Hang Múa steps. Start by seven — the roads are empty and the light is better.

Mai Châu EASY · FLAT

A flat valley floor ringed by mountains, with stilt-house villages strung along the lanes between rice fields. A couple of hours gets you around the whole thing. Bikes come free with most homestays.

Cát Bà Island MODERATE · HILLY

The island road climbs and drops through the national park, with a spur down to Việt Hải village. Short distances, real gradients, and coastal views most of the way.

Red River dykes EASY · FLAT

Raised embankment roads running out of Hanoi through market gardens and brick kilns. You can be out of the city in twenty minutes and into farmland that feels an hour away.

Worth knowingPù Luông is rideable but steep, and better on foot. The far-north loops — Hà Giang and Cao Bằng — are motorbike country: sustained climbs, hairpins, and heavy vehicle traffic on the passes. Go by car or easy-rider up there and save the bicycle for the valleys. Bring your own helmet if you're particular; rental ones are usually token.

Timing

Why October, and the one caveat

Early October is one of the two best windows in the northern year. The southwest monsoon has broken, Hanoi sits around 20–28°C with roughly nine rainy days across the whole month, trails are dry, and the rice harvest is finishing — which is what turns the terraces gold.

The caveatOctober is still inside typhoon season in the north. In October 2025 a storm crossed the Vietnam–China border on the 6th to 8th, Hanoi recorded over 200mm of rain in a day, and several northern provinces flooded. That was a severe year rather than a typical one, but it's a real reason to keep the closer options — Ninh Bình and Cát Bà, both within three hours — genuinely available, and to watch the forecast in the ten days beforehand.

Skip the centreHuế, Đà Nẵng and Hội An are in their wet season from September through December, at its worst in October and November. Hội An floods most years. It's also the standard tourist route, so nothing is lost by staying north.

Before you go

Staying out of trouble

Vietnam sits in the lowest advisory tier from both the US State Department and the UK FCDO. Violent crime against visitors is rare. The genuine risks, in order, are traffic, weather, and money.

This outweighs everything else on the list. Vietnam's road death rate is around 24.5 per 100,000, above the regional average.

  • Crossing on foot: walk at a slow, steady, predictable pace and don't stop. Traffic flows around you. Stopping or sprinting is what causes collisions.
  • On a bicycle: stay on rural lanes and dyke roads, ride defensively at junctions, and assume you have not been seen. Avoid national highways.
  • After dark: don't ride or cycle mountain roads at night, and avoid them in rain — the surface goes slick and the drops are unforgiving.

Phone and bag snatching from passing motorbikes is the most common incident with any physical risk attached — more frequent in Ho Chi Minh City but present in Hanoi.

  • Carry bags on the side away from traffic.
  • Don't stand at a curb navigating on your phone. Step back from the road first.
  • Crowded markets and the Old Quarter at night are where pickpocketing concentrates.
  • Fake e-visa sites. Convincing replicas of the government portal rank high in search results and either massively overcharge or harvest your identity. Type the official Immigration Department URL directly rather than clicking through a search.
  • Ghost accommodation. AI-generated listings for properties that don't exist, posted on otherwise legitimate booking platforms. Cross-check reviews and street view.
  • Taxis. Use Grab and confirm the driver's name and plate before getting in. Unlicensed drivers work airport arrivals hard.
  • Tampered QR codes at tourist sites, redirecting to phishing pages. Prefer cash or in-app payment.
  • Old Quarter touts, especially budget Ha Long cruise sellers around Tạ Hiện and Hoàn Kiếm. Book through your accommodation.
  • "It's closed today." It isn't. Walk past and check the gate.
  • Dengue is endemic. DEET repellent, particularly at dawn and dusk and in rural areas.
  • Hanoi air quality is poor in autumn. Check the index before long walks if either of you is asthmatic.
  • Bottled water. Ice in cities and established restaurants is generally fine.
  • Travel insurance with medical evacuation cover is not optional. Several of these places are hours from a serious hospital. Confirm the policy covers hiking and cycling.
  • In Hanoi, Family Medical Practice and Hồng Ngọc both treat foreign patients.

In the mountains the risk is water, not wind — flash floods and landslides. Never walk or drive through flood water, and take road closures at face value.

Cao Bằng and Hà Giang both run along the Chinese border. Carry your passport, carry the permit where one is required, stay on marked routes near the frontier, and note that drone use is restricted in border zones.
